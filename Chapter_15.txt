This approach radically simplifies the whole bargaining process by ignoring the opponent's

if the opponent happens to make a mistake. Moving on to section 4, the monotonic concession
protocol and simultaneous bidding. What actually happens when we change the rules
so both players have to act at the exact same time? This brings us to the monotonic concession
protocol. Instead of taking turns like we saw in Rubenstein's model, agents propose deals
simultaneously right in round one. They then compare the deals. If their proposals overlap
or match, boom, agreement is reached. If not, they move to the next round. In round T plus one,
each agent has a choice to make. They can either concede by making a proposal that is better for
the other agent than their previous offer, or they can simply wait and hold their ground.
The absolute core rule here, the monotonic part, is that no agent is ever allowed to backtrack.
You literally cannot make a proposal that the other agent prefers less than your previous offer.
And crucially, if you hit around where neither agent makes a concession,
the negotiation instantly terminates in that dreaded conflict deal. But there's a catch.
While those rules are super easy to verify, an agreement might not actually happen quickly.
In what we call task-oriented domains, or TODs, where agents are basically dividing up specific
chores or goals, the maximum number of rounds scales exponentially based on the number of tasks.
That's represented mathematically by O of 2 to the power of T. So a complex negotiation
could theoretically drag on for a massive, exponential number of rounds before finally
resolving. Which leads us to Section 5, the Zutton Strategy and Measuring Risk.
To navigate all this simultaneous bidding efficiently and avoid an endless staring contest,
agents can use a highly specific mathematical tactic called the Zutton Strategy.
So the Zutton Strategy dictates that an agent continuously measures its willingness to risk
conflict. Intuitively, an agent is way more willing to risk a conflict if the utility
difference between their current proposal and the dreaded conflict deal is very low.
They simply have less to lose. Under this strategy, an agent will concede, if and literally
only if, its calculated willingness to risk conflict is smaller than or equal to the other
agent's risk. And when they do concede, they don't just completely cave in. They concede precisely
enough to make the other agent's willingness to risk conflict value less than their own,
which mathematically flips all the pressure right back onto the opponent.
But, you know, mathematical models sometimes run into tricky edge cases. Like,

completely equal willingness to risk conflict? The strategy dictates both should concede.
But if agents know this, an intelligent agent might try to defect by holding firm,

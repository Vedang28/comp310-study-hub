Question 1. The flaws of plurality.
Let's start with the most common, yet maybe the most mathematically flawed voting method
we use.
Ok, so here's our baseline scenario. A group of friends is just trying to pick a movie
to watch together. Simple enough, right? They've got four genres to choose from. Action, romance,
comedy, and drama. Take a look at their preference schedule here. It maps out not just their
absolute top pick, but exactly how they rank their second, third, and fourth choices too.
Now, the easiest way to resolve this is a plurality vote. Essentially, we're running
a social choice function, which is just a fancy mathematical term for a process that
takes everyone's individual preferences and spits out a single group decision. Under plurality,
each candidate gets exactly one point for every preference order that ranks them first.
We completely, 100% ignore the rest of their carefully ranked choices.
So looking back at our table, comedy is the clear winner under plurality. It snags five
first place votes, beating actions 3, dramas 3, and romances 2. But, mathematically speaking,
this result is incredibly shallow. Because it completely ignores the bottom three rows
of our schedule, it totally wipes out all the rich nuance of how everyone actually feels
about those other options.
This brings us to a system designed specifically to fix exactly that shallow outcome. The board
account solves these plurality anomalies by doing exactly what plurality refuses to do.
It actually looks at the whole preference order. Here's how it works. For each candidate,
we create a numeric variable that basically counts the strength of opinion in its favor.
We increment the top choice by a set number of points, and then we just decrease that
value for subsequent choices down the ranks all the way until the final, last place outcome
gets zero points.
Let's see this point increment math in action. Take candidate omega 2 here as an example.
Voter 1 ranks them first, granting two points. But then, voters 2 and 3 rank them second,
giving one point each. So, the board account for omega 2 becomes 4. Suddenly, those second
and third place votes are heavily impacting the final total. It gives us a way better
reflection of the group's overall satisfaction, you know?
Section 3. Alternative vote and runoffs. What if we want to guarantee our winner actually
has a genuine majority, like over 50%, rather than just a high aggregate point score?
Enter the alternative vote, also known as instant runoff voting, or IRV. Just like the
board account, voters rank the candidates. But the counting procedure? Totally different.
We count in rounds. Every round, the last place candidate gets eliminated, and we keep
going until someone captures a true majority. It's a really elegant solution to weird anomalies
like Condorcet's paradox. You know, that frustrating scenario where group preferences
loop in a cycle? Like A beats B, B beats C, but C beats A? IRV just breaks that cycle
by systematically tossing out the least popular options.
Let's run this through a slightly larger movie voting scenario, this time with 23 voters.
So a majority here means hitting 12 or more votes. In round one, we only look at first
choice votes. Action has 7, comedy has 10, drama 2, and horror 4. Nobody has 12. Since
drama got the fewest votes, it gets completely eliminated, and its votes are going to be
reallocated.
Moving into round two, we take those two votes that originally went to drama, and we look
at those voters' second choices. Their second choice was horror, so we hand those two votes
right over to horror. But even with that little bump, horror now falls into last place with
only 6 combined votes. Since nobody has hit that magic number of 12 yet, horror is the
next one knocked out.
Finally, we hit round three, and we redistribute horror's 6 votes. Looking at the schedules,
two of those votes slide over to comedy, and four slide over to action. And boom, this
pushes comedy to a total of 12 votes. Comedy hits the exact 50 plus percent mark to secure
the win. Through instant runoffs, we actually found a winner that the majority of the room
can at least tolerate.
Question 4. What makes a good vote? Seeing all these wildly different mechanics, we really
have to ask, mathematically speaking, what are the properties of a genuinely good voting
procedure?
Well, when computer scientists classify an ideal voting system, they're generally looking
for three key conditions to be met. The Pareto property, the Condorcet winner condition,
and the independence of irrelevant alternatives, or IIA for short. These right here are actually
the foundation for the famous Arrow's impossibility theorem.
Let's break these down really quick. The Pareto property says that if everybody prefers
candidate A over candidate B, then A absolutely must be ranked over B in the final outcome.
Makes sense, right? Then, the Condorcet winner condition means if a candidate would win every
single head-to-head matchup against all the others, they must be ranked first. IIA dictates
that the ranking between A and B shouldn't mysteriously flip just because candidate C
enters or leaves the race. And obviously, no single agent's preferences should ever
strictly dictate the outcome. Basically, no dictators allowed.
But here is the brutal reality of Arrow's theorem for your exams. It mathematically
proves that no rank order voting system can ever meet all these fairness criteria at the
same time. Mathematical perfection is literally impossible.
Section 5. The Dictator Theorem. Let's see what happens to the math when we introduce
a little thing called human nature. Because of human nature, we always have to worry about
tactical voting. You know, where someone strategically lies on their ballot just to get a better
overall result for themselves. So this leads to the ultimate theoretical question. Can
we design a social choice function that is completely immune to manipulation? Like, can
we build a system where lying never pays off? According to game theory and the deep math
of multi-agent systems, the answer is a resounding, unavoidable no. It literally does not exist.
This right here is the harsh reality of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem. It mathematically
proves that for elections with more than two candidates, the only perfectly non-manipulable
system is a literal dictatorship. Every other system can be gamed. But there is a silver
lining that you'll definitely need to know for your exams. Computer scientists fight
back using computational complexity. Basically, by making the voting algorithm so incredibly
complex that it is NP-hard to calculate how to effectively lie, we can make manipulation
practically impossible in the real world, even if it's theoretically possible on paper.
Knowing that every single voting system has these fatal theoretical flaws, whether it's
plurality ignoring nuance, or ranked systems being vulnerable to tactical lies, we just
have to accept that group decisions are always a mathematical compromise.

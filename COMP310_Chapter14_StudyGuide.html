Chapter 14: Allocating Scarce Resources

Auctions, Bidding Languages, Winner Determination & the VCG Mechanism

1. Overview

Allocating scarce resources among competing agents is a central problem in multi-agent systems. When demand exceeds supply, we need a mechanism to decide who gets what and at what price.

What is a Scarce Resource?

  • A physical object (e.g., artwork, equipment)
  • The right to use land or spectrum
  • Computational resources (processor time, memory, bandwidth)

If the resource isn't scarce, or there's no competition, allocation is trivial. When there is greater demand than supply, we turn to auctions — market institutions where traders exchange price information through bids and asks.

Why Auctions?

Auctions grew massively with the internet (frictionless commerce). eBay, Google AdWords auctions — things that weren't previously profitable to auction are now routine. Auctions are effective at allocating resources efficiently and can reveal truths about bidders.

2. What is an Auction?

An auction takes place between an agent called the auctioneer and a collection of agents called the bidders. The goal is to allocate a good (the scarce resource) to one of the bidders.

Key Terminology

  • Traders — participants in the auction with allocations of goods and money
  • Bid — an offer to buy at a given price
  • Ask — an offer to sell at a given price
  • Exchange — the free alteration of allocations of goods and money between traders
  • Limit price — the value or maximum price a trader places on the good (affects behaviour)

4. Types of Value

Different auction models embody different assumptions about how bidders value the good:

Private Value

The good has a value to me that is independent of what it is worth to you. Example: John Lennon's last dollar bill — its sentimental value is purely personal.

Common Value

The good has the same value to all of us, but we have differing estimates of what it is. This leads to the winner's curse: the winning bid in an auction exceeds the intrinsic value or true worth of the item.

Correlated Value

Our values are related. The more you're prepared to pay, the more I should be prepared to pay. A middle ground between private and common value.

The Winner's Curse

With common-value goods, the winner tends to be the bidder who overestimated the true value the most. Winning itself is evidence of overpaying! Each trader has a limit price that affects their bidding behaviour.

5. English Auction

The classic auction format everyone knows — the "going once, going twice" style.

How It Works

  • Buyers call out ascending bids (prices increase)
  • The auctioneer may call out prices with buyers indicating agreement
  • A reserve price may be set (the minimum acceptable price)
  • Ends at a fixed time (internet auctions) or when no more bidding activity
  • The "last man standing" pays their bid

6. Dutch Auction

Also called a "descending clock" auction. Some auctions literally use a clock to display the prices.

How It Works

  • Starts at a high price, auctioneer calls out descending prices
  • One bidder claims the good by indicating the current price is acceptable
  • Winner pays the price at which they "stop the clock"
  • Ties are broken by restarting from a slightly higher price
PropertyClassification
PriceFirst-price
VisibilityOpen-cry
DirectionDescending
Use CasePerishable goods: flowers (Netherlands), fish (Spain/Israel), tobacco (Canada)
SpeedHigh volume — auctions proceed swiftly

7. First-Price Sealed-Bid (FPSB)

In a sealed-bid auction, none of the open-cry dynamics happen. You learn nothing about the market. At most, you know the winning price after the auction.

How It Works

  • Each bidder submits a single sealed bid
  • The highest bid wins
  • Winner pays their own bid (first-price)
PropertyClassification
PriceFirst-price
VisibilitySealed bid
RoundsOne-shot
Use CaseTreasury bonds (UK), property (Scotland)

The Problem: Strategic Bidding

Because you pay exactly what you bid, bidders are incentivised to shade their bids below their true valuation — constantly second-guessing each other to avoid overpaying. This makes the auction inefficient.

Show Transcript — Sealed-Bid Problem
But contrast that with a first-price sealed bid system. In that scenario, everyone slides their offer into a secret envelope. You learn absolutely nothing about the rest of the market. The highest bidder just wins, and they pay exactly what they offered. The massive problem here? It's strategic bidding. Players are constantly second-guessing each other, just to avoid paying too much.

8. Vickrey Auction

The Vickrey auction is a second-price sealed-bid auction, devised by William Vickrey (Nobel Prize winner). It adds a single brilliant twist that changes the entire game.

The Vickrey Rule

  • Each bidder submits a single sealed bid
  • Bidders do not see the bids made by others
  • The highest bid wins
  • But the winner pays the amount of the second-highest bid, not their own
PropertyClassification
PriceSecond-price
VisibilitySealed bid
RoundsOne-shot
Use CaseStamps, paper collectibles, online ad auctions

Key Result

In a Vickrey auction, it is a dominant strategy for each bidder to bid their true valuation. No mind games required — the math enforces honesty.

So picture a one-shot, sealed bid environment. Historically, this format was super common for selling things like rare stamps or paper collectibles. But then, William Vickrey, who actually won a Nobel prize for this, came along and added a single brilliant twist to the rules that honestly changed everything. Mathematically, it makes it in the bidder's absolute best interest to just bid the true, honest value. No mind games required.

9. Why Does the Vickrey Auction Work?

It sounds counterintuitive — why would a seller accept less money? The answer lies in the payoff structure that makes lying pointless.

Payoff for bidder i
pi = { vi − maxj≠i bj   if bi > maxj≠i bj   |   0   otherwise }

Where vi is bidder i's true value and bi is their bid. If you win, your payoff is your value minus the second-highest bid. If you lose, your payoff is 0.

Intuition: Two Ways to Lie

  • Bid more than your valuation → might win but risk negative payoff
  • Bid less than your valuation → risk losing, but if you win you pay the same anyway

Neither deviation can ever make you better off than bidding truthfully. At best, they give the same result. At worst, they hurt you.

10. Proof: Overbidding Doesn't Help

Assume bidder i bids bi > vi (overbids). We analyse three cases based on where the second-highest bid falls:

Case Analysis: Overbidding (bi > vi)

Case 1: maxj≠i bj < vi

The bidder would win whether or not the bid was truthful. Both strategies give the same payoff: vi − maxj≠i bj.

Result: No benefit from overbidding

Case 2: maxj≠i bj > bi

The bidder would lose regardless — the other bid is higher than even the inflated bid. Both strategies have equal payoffs (zero).

Result: No benefit from overbidding

Case 3: vi < maxj≠i bj < bi

Overbidding wins the auction, but the payoff is negative (vi − maxj≠i bj < 0). Truthful bidding would have lost and paid zero.

Result: Overbidding is WORSE

Conclusion

Overbidding can only give an equal or worse outcome compared to truthful bidding. It is never strictly better.

Show Transcript — Overbidding Proof
First up, let's assume you try to game the whole system by overbidding. Mathematically speaking, your bid is strictly greater than your true internal valuation of the item. If you overbid, a couple of things could happen. First, sure, you might win the auction. But because you artificially inflated your bid, the second highest bid might actually be higher than what you personally think the item is worth. If that happens, yeah, you win, but you pay a price that leaves you with a negative payoff. You literally lose money. Or, alternatively, the other bids might be so low that you would have won anyway just by telling the truth, or they're so high that you still lose regardless. The big takeaway here? Deviating by overbidding can only ever give you an equal or worse outcome than just telling the truth.

11. Proof: Underbidding Doesn't Help

Assume bidder i bids bi < vi (underbids). Again, three cases:

Case Analysis: Underbidding (bi < vi)

Case 1: maxj≠i bj > vi

The bidder would lose regardless — the highest other bid exceeds even the true valuation. Both strategies give zero payoff.

Result: No benefit from underbidding

Case 2: maxj≠i bj < bi

The bidder would win either way. Both strategies give the same payoff.

Result: No benefit from underbidding

Case 3: bi < maxj≠i bj < vi

Truthful bidding would have won with a positive payoff (vi − maxj≠i bj > 0). But underbidding loses, earning zero.

Result: Underbidding is WORSE

Final Conclusion: Truthful Bidding is Dominant

Neither overbidding nor underbidding can ever improve your outcome. Truthful bidding (bi = vi) is a weakly dominant strategy in the Vickrey auction.

Show Transcript — Underbidding Proof
What if you decide to play it sneaky and underbid? In this scenario, your bid is actually less than your true valuation. Well, by artificially lowering your bid, you're mathematically just giving yourself a worse chance of winning overall. You could easily lose an auction that you actually would have won if you'd just been truthful. But let's say you underbid and you still manage to win. Does it help? No. Because of the Vickrey Rule, you still pay the exact same second highest price anyway. Your lie literally didn't save you a single penny. Truthful bidding as a dominant strategy is practically enforced by the math.

12. Collusion & Real-World Flaws

While the math is elegant, the Vickrey auction is not a panacea. None of the auction types discussed are immune to collusion.

Real-World Case Study

New Zealand Spectrum Auction

When New Zealand sold radio spectrum rights using a Vickrey-style auction, interdependencies between licenses led to strategic bidding. One firm bid NZ$100,000 for a license — but the second-highest bid was only NZ$6. They paid $6 for a $100,000 asset.

Bidder Collusion

A grand coalition of bidders can agree beforehand to:

  • Propose artificially low bids for a good
  • Obtain the true value for a good
  • Share the profit afterwards

Shill Bidding

An auctioneer could employ bogus bidders (shills) to artificially increase bids in open-cry auctions. This can result in the winner's curse — winning actually means you've been tricked into overpaying.

Show Transcript — Collusion
When the New Zealand government tried to sell off radio spectrum rights using a Vickrey-style auction, they hit a massive wall because of how interdependent those rights were. One firm actually bid $100,000 New Zealand dollars for a license. But because of strategic behavior and a complete lack of competing bids for that one isolated frequency, the second highest bid was literally $6. They paid $6 for a $100,000 asset. It was a shocking discrepancy, and it really highlighted that these auction formats aren't just some magical cure-all. An auctioneer might sneak in bogus bidders, which are known as shills, specifically to artificially inflate that second highest bid.

13. Combinatorial Auctions

A combinatorial auction is an auction for bundles of goods, not just single items. This is essential when items have interdependent values.

Motivating Example

Spectrum Licenses

For the 1.7–1.72 GHz bandwidth for Brooklyn to be useful, you need a license for Manhattan, Queens, and Staten Island too. The most valuable licenses cover the same bandwidth. A different bandwidth license is better than nothing, but more expensive.

Formal Setup

  • Define a set of items to be auctioned: Z = {z1, ..., zm}
  • A set of agents: Ag = {1, ..., n}
  • Each agent i has a valuation function over bundles

14. Valuation Functions

Each agent's preferences are given by a valuation function:

Valuation Function
vi : 2Z → ℝ

For every possible bundle of goods Z ⊆ Z, vi(Z) says how much Z is worth to agent i.

Key Properties

  • Normalised: vi(∅) = 0 — an empty allocation is worth nothing
  • Free disposal: Z1 ⊆ Z2 implies vi(Z1) ≤ vi(Z2) — more stuff is never worse

The Representation Problem

Specifying all preferences is exponential: vi : 2Z → ℝ. For 1122 items, you'd need to specify 21122 values per bidder — computationally intractable. This is why we need bidding languages.

15. Allocation of Goods & Social Welfare

An outcome for a combinatorial auction is an allocation — a list of sets Z1, ..., Zn, one per agent.

Allocation Rules

  • Each Zi ⊆ Z (each agent gets a subset of goods)
  • For all i ≠ j: Zi ∩ Zj = ∅ (no good allocated to more than one agent)
  • Not all goods need to be allocated

The set of all such allocations is denoted alloc(Z, Ag).

Social Welfare Function

A desirable property is to maximise social welfare — the sum of all agents' utilities:

Social Welfare
sw(Z1, ..., Zn, v1, ..., vn) = Σi=1n vi(Zi)

16. Winner Determination Problem

Given a set of goods Z and valuation functions v1, ..., vn, the goal is to find the allocation Z1*, ..., Zn* that maximises social welfare:

Winner Determination
Z1*, ..., Zn* = argmax(Z1,...,Zn) ∈ alloc(Z,Ag) sw(Z1, ..., Zn, v1, ..., vn)

The Hat Notation: v̂i

Each agent i declares a valuation v̂i. The hat (^) denotes that this is what the agent says, not necessarily what it truly is. Remember: an agent may lie!

Computational Complexity

Determining the winner is a combinatorial optimisation problem that is NP-hard. But this is a worst-case result — optimal or approximate approaches may work well in practice.

17. Bidding Languages

Rather than exhaustive evaluations of all 2|Z| bundles, bidding languages let bidders construct valuations from the bids they want to mention.

Atomic Bid

An atomic bid β is a pair (Z, p) where:

  • Z ⊆ Z is a set of items
  • p is the price the bidder is prepared to pay for bundle Z

A bundle Z' satisfies an atomic bid (Z, p) if Z ⊆ Z' (the bundle contains at least the items in the bid).

Atomic Bid Valuation
vβ(Z') = { p   if Z' satisfies (Z, p)   |   0   otherwise }

Atomic bids alone can't construct complex valuations — bidders need to combine them using XOR or OR connectives.

18. XOR Bids

With XOR bids, we pay for at most one of the bundles (exclusive or).

XOR Bid Definition

A bid β = (Z1, p1) XOR ... XOR (Zk, pk) defines:

XOR Valuation
vβ(Z') = { 0 if Z' doesn't satisfy any (Zi, pi)   |   max{pi | Zi ⊆ Z'} otherwise }
  • Pay nothing if the allocation doesn't satisfy any bid
  • Otherwise, pay the largest price of any satisfied bid
Worked Example

B1 = ({a,b}, 3) XOR ({c,d}, 5)

"I'll pay 3 for a bundle containing a and b but not c and d. I'll pay 5 for a bundle containing c and d but not a and b. And I'll pay 5 for a bundle containing a, b, c and d."

Bundle Z'vβ₁(Z')Reasoning
{a}0Neither {a,b} nor {c,d} is satisfied
{b}0Neither atomic bid satisfied
{a, b}3{a,b} ⊆ {a,b} — first bid satisfied
{c, d}5{c,d} ⊆ {c,d} — second bid satisfied
{a, b, c, d}5Both satisfied → max(3, 5) = 5 (XOR!)

XOR Bids are Fully Expressive

XOR bids can express any valuation function over a set of goods. However, this may require an exponentially large number of atomic bids. The valuation of a bundle can be computed in polynomial time.

19. OR Bids

With OR bids, we are prepared to pay for more than one bundle simultaneously.

OR Bid Definition

Given β = (Z1, p1) OR ... OR (Zk, pk), we find the set of atomic bids W contained in β such that:

  • Every bid in W is satisfied by Z'
  • Each pair of bids in W has mutually disjoint sets of goods
  • No other subset W' gives a higher total price
Worked Example

B1 = ({a,b}, 3) OR ({c,d}, 5)

"I'll pay 3 for a bundle with a and b. I'll pay 5 for c and d. And I'll pay 8 for both bundles together."

Bundle Z'vβ₁(Z')vs. XOR
{a}0Same
{b}0Same
{a, b}3Same
{c, d}5Same
{a, b, c, d}8Different! XOR gave 5

With OR, the last bundle costs 3 + 5 = 8 because we can pay for both bundles (disjoint goods).

OR Bids: Limitations

  • OR bids are strictly less expressive than XOR bids
  • Some valuations cannot be expressed, e.g.: v({a}) = 1, v({b}) = 1, v({a,b}) = 1
  • Computing vβ(Z) for an OR bid is NP-hard

20. Winner Determination via ILP

A typical approach is to formulate winner determination as an Integer Linear Programming (ILP) problem and use a standard solver.

ILP Formulation
maximize Σi∈Ag, Z⊆Z xi,Z · vi(Z)

Subject to Constraints

  1. Each good allocated at most once: Σi∈Ag, Z⊆Z | z∈Z xi,Z ≤ 1   for all z ∈ Z
  2. Each agent gets at most one bundle: ΣZ⊆Z xi,Z ≤ 1   for all i ∈ Ag
  3. Non-negativity: xi,Z ≥ 0   for all i ∈ Ag, Z ⊆ Z

Alternative Approaches

  • Approximation algorithms — guaranteed to terminate quickly with a result within a known distance from optimal (but few have been found within reasonable bounds)
  • Heuristic solutions — greedy allocations: find the largest bid we can satisfy, then the next largest, etc.

21. The VCG Mechanism

Auctions are easy to strategically manipulate — in general, we don't know if agents' declared valuations are true. The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism solves this.

Incentive Compatible

A mechanism is incentive compatible if telling the truth is a dominant strategy. VCG is a generalisation of the Vickrey auction to combinatorial settings — no agent can benefit by declaring anything other than its true valuation.

How VCG Works

1
Every agent i simultaneously declares a valuation function v̂i (may not be true)
2
The mechanism computes the allocation Z1*, ..., Zn* that maximises social welfare based on declared valuations
3
Every agent i pays pi — the "compensation" for the utility that other agents lose by agent i participating

Key Notation

  • v0 — the indifferent valuation: v0(Z) = 0 for all Z (a bid that doesn't care about any goods)
  • sw−i — social welfare function without agent i: sw−i(Z1, ..., Zn, v1, ..., vn) = Σj∈Ag, j≠i vj(Zj)

22. VCG Payment Formula

Each agent i pays the difference in social welfare to others between the world without them and the world with them.

Step 1: Compute the "without i" allocation

Let Z1', ..., Zn' be the allocation that would have been chosen if agent i declared v0 (indifferent) and every other agent declared the same:

Allocation Without Agent i
Z1', ..., Zn' = argmax(Z1,...,Zn) ∈ alloc(Z,Ag) sw(Z1, ..., Zn, v̂1, ..., v0, ..., v̂n)

Step 2: Compute the payment

VCG Payment for Agent i
pi = sw−i(Z1', ..., Zn', v̂1, ..., v0, ..., v̂n) − sw−i(Z1*, ..., Zn*, v̂1, ..., v̂i, ..., v̂n)

In Plain English

pi = (how well everyone else does when i doesn't participate) − (how well everyone else does when i does participate)

You pay for the harm your participation causes to other agents.

Sanity Check

VCG with a Single Item = Vickrey Auction

With a singleton bundle, the only agent that pays anything is the one with the highest bid. If they hadn't participated, the agent with the second-highest bid would have won. Therefore, agent i compensates by paying the second-highest bid — exactly the Vickrey rule!

Why VCG is a Game Changer

  • Incentive compatible: no agent benefits from lying — same reasoning as Vickrey proof
  • Social welfare maximisation can be implemented in dominant strategies
  • Called "a rare ray of sunshine on the problems of multi-agent interaction"
Show Transcript — VCG Mechanism
To tackle this massive headache, economists came up with the Vickrey Clark Groves, or VCG mechanism. It basically takes the enforced honesty of the Vickrey auction and scales it all the way up to this insane combinatorial level. Here's how it works. In a VCG auction, each agent pays the cost to the other agents of having participated in the auction at all. Think of it like this. You are basically paying for the harm your winning causes everyone else by taking those specific items off the board. If you were to simplify VCG down to just a single item, the math completely proves the winning agent pays exactly the second highest bid, meaning it perfectly mirrors the Vickrey auction. There is absolutely zero benefit for an agent to declare anything other than their true valuation, and it mathematically guarantees that the final outcome will maximize overall social welfare.
